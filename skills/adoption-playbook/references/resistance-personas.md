# Resistance Personas — who pushes back, and what actually converts them

Resistance is data, not disloyalty. Each persona has a real concern under the stated one; address the real one.

## The IDE Pirate (senior, high-status, "I'm not giving up my setup")

- **Stated:** the tool threatens a finely-tuned workflow. **Real:** status — mastery of the current stack *is* their standing.
- **Convert:** correct the false premise quietly (Claude Code runs in the terminal beside any editor — nobody is taking the IDE). Recruit the ringleader as a design partner: early access, ownership of the "expert workflow" standards for their repos. Position mastery of the new tool as elite skill. Never mandate — you'd convert your most influential engineer into your most influential detractor.
- **Don't block the timeline on them:** sequence their guild later and let published peer results do the arguing.

## The Show-Me Skeptic (staff/principal, "try it on our legacy estate and get back to me")

- **Stated:** it won't work on our COBOL / Basel calc / matching engine. **Real:** they've watched tool mandates fail for 15 years and are protecting their team's time.
- **Convert:** evidence on *their* code — a scoped evaluation on the hard estate with published, honest results (including where it fell short). Fund one skeptic-led evaluation; a converted show-me skeptic becomes the program's best champion. Honesty about limits is the entire currency here.

## The Anxious Mid-Career Dev ("is this replacing me?")

- **Stated:** quality concerns. **Real:** job security and identity.
- **Convert:** an honest narrative from leadership about what roles look like in 12 months (orchestration, review, higher-order design — and say plainly what is and isn't planned for headcount). Career framing: AI-fluency as promotable skill. Champions who model the transition matter more than any memo. Evasive comms are worse than hard truths — engineers pattern-match evasion instantly.

## The Privacy-Wary Junior ("I'll use it if my manager doesn't see my prompt logs")

- **Stated & real are the same:** surveillance fear.
- **Convert:** an explicit, published log-access policy — who can access prompt logs, for what purpose (audit/security, not performance management), with what controls. Cohort-level metrics only; never individual usage in manager dashboards. In EU orgs this doubles as works-council homework. If the program can't answer "who reads my prompts?", adoption among juniors quietly dies.

## The Squeezed EM (owns delivery dates, sees only disruption)

- **Stated:** "we don't have slack this quarter." **Real:** they're accountable for dates and get no credit for adoption.
- **Convert:** make adoption help their goals — count enablement time in planning, target a team-level win on their actual backlog in week 1, give EMs cohort dashboards so they see the payback. Middle managers are the layer that makes or breaks adoption; a program with exec sponsorship and IC enthusiasm still dies in an unsupportive middle.

## The Blanket-No Security Reviewer

- **Stated:** policy says no. **Real:** they've been burned by tools adopted around them, and they own the blast radius.
- **Convert:** bring them in as an author, not an approver-of-record. Enterprise controls (zero-retention terms, egress wiring, audit logs, managed settings) answer most objections — walk them through, then co-write the policy tiers. Security people who help design the guardrails defend them; security people who are handed a fait accompli block it.

## Program rules across personas

1. Opt-in beats mandate everywhere the mandate isn't structurally required — earned adoption sticks, coerced adoption reverts.
2. Convert the influential, publish the results, let social proof carry the middle.
3. Every persona's objection, taken seriously, improves the program (the pirate hardens workflows, the skeptic builds the evidence base, the junior forces the privacy policy). Treat resistance as free program QA.
